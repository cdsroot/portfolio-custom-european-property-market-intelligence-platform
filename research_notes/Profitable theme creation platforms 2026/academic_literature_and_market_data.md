## A1. Platform / two-sided market economics and platform-owner entry: what it implies for complementors (theme and template sellers)

### Takeaway
The empirical literature does not give one sign for platform-owner entry: Amazon and Google entry studies find the owner targets successful complementor niches and discourages affected sellers, while the Google Photos study finds entry raised complementor innovation through attention spillovers. The one study on Shopify itself (apps, not themes; a 2026 arXiv preprint) finds Shopify's first-party entry re-concentrated a category and cut the leading third-party app's growth by about three quarters, and that most apps get no detectable installs.

### Cited Findings

**Theory (no effect sizes; foundational only)**
- Rochet, J.-C. & Tirole, J. (2003). "Platform Competition in Two-Sided Markets." *Journal of the European Economic Association* 1(4): 990–1029. DOI 10.1162/154247603322493212. The paper builds a model of platform competition with two-sided markets and compares outcomes across governance structures (profit-maximising platforms, not-for-profit joint undertakings) against an integrated monopolist and a Ramsey planner. Theoretical; reports no empirical effect sizes. [ABSTRACT/RECORD] — [OUP journal page](https://academic.oup.com/jeea/article-abstract/1/4/990/2280902); [EconPapers record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:tpr:jeurec:v:1:y:2003:i:4:p:990-1029)
- Parker, G. G. & Van Alstyne, M. W. (2005). "Two-Sided Network Effects: A Theory of Information Product Design." *Management Science* 51(10): 1494–1504. DOI 10.1287/mnsc.1050.0400. Formal model showing a firm may rationally invest in a product it intends to give away permanently, even without competition; either side of the market (content providers or end consumers) can be the one receiving the free good. Theoretical; no effect sizes. [ABSTRACT/RECORD] — [EconPapers record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:ormnsc:v:51:y:2005:i:10:p:1494-1504); [INFORMS page](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/mnsc.1050.0400)

**Complementor crowding**
- Boudreau, K. J. (2012). "Let a Thousand Flowers Bloom? An Early Look at Large Numbers of Software App Developers and Patterns of Innovation." *Organization Science* 23(5): 1409–1427. DOI 10.1287/orsc.1110.0678. Setting: app producers on leading handheld (PDA) platforms, 1999–2004. Findings as stated in the abstract: a tight link between the number of producers and the number of software varieties; later cohorts generated less compelling software than earlier cohorts; adding producers of differentiated apps raised innovation incentives while adding producers of close substitutes reduced them; crowding of similar apps dominated, so the average effect of adding producers on innovation incentives was negative. The abstract gives no percentage effect sizes. [ABSTRACT/RECORD] — [EconPapers record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:ororsc:v:23:y:2012:i:5:p:1409-1427); [SSRN working version](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=1826702)

**Platform-owner entry**
- Zhu, F. & Liu, Q. (2018). "Competing with complementors: An empirical look at Amazon.com." *Strategic Management Journal* 39(10): 2618–2642. DOI 10.1002/smj.2932. Findings (abstract): Amazon's likelihood of entering a product space is positively correlated with the popularity and customer ratings of third-party sellers' products; entry reduces shipping costs and increases demand for affected products; small affected third-party sellers appear discouraged from growing their businesses on the platform. [ABSTRACT/RECORD] — [EconPapers SMJ record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:stratm:v:39:y:2018:i:10:p:2618-2642); [HBS working paper abstract](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:hbs:wpaper:15-044)
- Same paper, working-paper version (data collected June 2013 and April 2014): "Amazon entered 3% of these complementors' product spaces over a 10-month period"; Amazon was more likely to enter when third-party products had higher sales and better reviews and did not use Amazon's fulfilment service. The 3% figure is from the working-paper text; I did not check whether the published SMJ version reports the same number. [FULL TEXT of working paper] — [Wharton Mack Institute copy](https://mackinstitute.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2015/04/Zhu-Feng-Liu-Qihong_Competing-with-Complementors.-An-Empirical-Look-at-Amazon.com_.pdf)
- Wen, W. & Zhu, F. (2019). "Threat of platform-owner entry and complementor responses: Evidence from the mobile app market." *Strategic Management Journal* 40(9): 1336–1367. DOI 10.1002/smj.3031. Setting: Android app developers facing Google's entry threat (difference-in-differences). Findings (abstract): after Google's entry threat increases, affected developers reduce innovation and raise prices for the affected apps, and shift innovation to unaffected and new apps; the authors note entry may reduce wasteful duplicate development while pushing developers toward possibly less lucrative areas. The abstract contains no percentages. [ABSTRACT/RECORD] — [EconPapers record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:stratm:v:40:y:2019:i:9:p:1336-1367)
- Foerderer, J., Kude, T., Mithas, S. & Heinzl, A. (2018). "Does Platform Owner's Entry Crowd Out Innovation? Evidence from Google Photos." *Information Systems Research* 29(2): 444–460. DOI 10.1287/isre.2018.0787. Setting: Google's 2015 entry into photography apps on Android; panel of 6,620 apps. Finding: entry was associated with a 9.6% increase in the likelihood of major updates for affected apps relative to similar unaffected apps; the mechanism was increased consumer attention to the category rather than competitive racing; the effect was more pronounced for larger and more diversified complementors. [SNIPPET for the 9.6% and 6,620 figures; record confirmed] — [IDEAS record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/orisre/v29y2018i2p444-460.html); [SSRN version](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2805510)
- Li, Z. & Agarwal, A. (2017). "Platform Integration and Demand Spillovers in Complementary Markets: Evidence from Facebook's Integration of Instagram." *Management Science* 63(10): 3438–3458. DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2016.2502. Finding (abstract): Instagram's growth after integration spilled over positively to big third-party photo-sharing apps and negatively to small ones; overall demand for the photo-sharing ecosystem rose. No percentages retrieved. [ABSTRACT/RECORD] — [EconPapers record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:ormnsc:v:63:y:2017:i:10:p:3438-3458)

**Shopify-specific (apps, not themes) — the closest setting to theme sellers**
- Assabese, F., Celis, P.-J. & Destefanis, G. (2026). "Scale, Concentration, and Entry Timing in the Shopify App Ecosystem: A Longitudinal Study of Platform Governance and Application Survival." arXiv:2608.23771 (v1, 24 Aug 2026, cs.SE). **Preprint, not peer-reviewed.** Data: September 2025 snapshot of all 24,826 apps; weekly panel of 7,708 apps over 366 weeks (Feb 2019–Mar 2026); adoption measured as installations detected on live storefronts by Store Leads (not revenue). [FULL TEXT] — [arXiv abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.23771); [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.23771)
  - Adoption skew: of 16,698 active apps, 74.8% (12,485) recorded zero detected installations; only 4,213 apps had at least one; the median install count was zero and the 75th percentile was 2. The authors note this partly reflects detection limits of web scraping. — [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.23771)
  - Category concentration (55 functional categories, installation shares): mean HHI 0.190, median 0.144; mean top-5 app share 71.2% (median 71.1%); 11 categories (20.0%) high concentration (HHI ≥ 0.25), 16 (29.1%) moderate, 28 (50.9%) low; larger categories consistently less concentrated. Most concentrated: social proof, HHI 0.678, leading app 81.9%, top five 93.6%. — [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.23771)
  - Platform-owner entry: before the July 2021 relaunch of Shopify Inbox, the chat category had been de-concentrating (HHI 0.51 → 0.29 from Feb 2019); after entry the category HHI rose to a peak of 0.55, Inbox peaked just under 400,000 installations in March 2024 and held 67.8% of the category in the Sept 2025 snapshot; Tidio, the leading third-party incumbent, fell from a median of +193 installations per week in the 26 weeks before to +48 in the 26 weeks after. The authors say a permutation check tempers this result. — [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.23771)
  - Platform exit: the deprecation of Shopify's own Product Reviews app released 191,000 installations, of which at most a third reappeared as competitor adoption within a year. — [arXiv abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.23771)
  - Entry timing: later entrants grew faster than early movers in 88% of 50 categories analysed, robust across seven years of quarterly re-estimation; early movers on net lost detected installations; category leadership changed hands in 92% of categories over seven years. — [arXiv abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.23771)
  - Failure: the median exiting app peaked at 8 detected installations and disappeared from tracking within 68 weeks of launch; first-six-month public data predicts exit within two years with cross-validated AUC above 0.8. — [arXiv abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.23771)

### Inferences
- By analogy only: Shopify, Wix, Squarespace, Webflow and Framer shipping first-party free themes and AI site generators is a platform-owner-entry event of the kind studied by Zhu & Liu (Amazon) and Wen & Zhu (Google). Those studies predict the owner targets the most popular niches and that affected complementors shift effort elsewhere or stop growing on the platform. None of these papers measured theme sellers.
- The Foerderer et al. result is the counter-case: entry can raise attention to a whole category. It applied to larger, diversified complementors, which by analogy suggests any spillover benefit from platform-made themes would favour established theme studios rather than new solo sellers. This is my extrapolation, not a finding.
- The Shopify app paper's "later entrants outgrow early movers in 88% of categories" and "leadership changed hands in 92% of categories" imply incumbency on Shopify is less protective than in classic winner-take-all models. Whether this transfers to the Shopify Theme Store (a much smaller, curated catalogue with one-time purchases) is untested.
- Store Leads data in section B2 shows the ten most-used Shopify themes account for about 55% of all detected Shopify stores; to my knowledge all ten names are first-party Shopify themes. That is direct descriptive evidence consistent with platform-owner dominance of the theme layer, but the free/paid attribution is my own knowledge, not stated on the Store Leads page.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed or working-paper study of platform-owner entry into themes/templates (Shopify free themes, Wix/Framer AI generators, WordPress default/block themes) was found.
- Kapacinskaite & Mostajabi (2024, *Strategic Management Journal*, "Competing with the platform: Complementor positioning and …") surfaced in search ([Bocconi IRIS copy](https://iris.unibocconi.it/retrieve/2ca35557-f1a7-4f52-80f2-51b28d96755e/Strategic%20Management%20Journal%20-%202024%20-%20Kapacinskaite%20-%20Competing%20with%20the%20platform%20%20Complementor%20positioning%20and.pdf)) but I did not retrieve its full title, DOI or findings; do not cite beyond its existence.
- The published SMJ version of Zhu & Liu and the published ISR version of Foerderer et al. were not read in full (publisher pages are paywalled); figures come from the working paper and index summaries respectively.

## A2. Earnings concentration in digital-goods marketplaces: superstar vs long-tail evidence

### Takeaway
Every dataset found shows extreme skew: the top-ranked paid iPhone app got about 150 times the downloads of the app ranked 200th, three quarters of Shopify apps have no detectable installs, and the only bottom-up ThemeForest analysis (a 2016 industry blog, not academic) estimated 76% of WordPress themes earned under $1,000 a month. No peer-reviewed study quantifies revenue concentration for themes, Steam, npm or VS Code extensions.

### Cited Findings

**Peer-reviewed / NBER**
- Rosen, S. (1981). "The Economics of Superstars." *American Economic Review* 71(5): 845–858. Theoretical paper: superstar-level rewards arise from imperfect substitution among sellers of differing quality combined with scale economies of joint consumption. No empirical concentration statistics. I confirmed the citation through secondary pages only; I did not open the AER/JSTOR record. [SNIPPET] — [Minneapolis Fed article summarising Rosen](https://www.minneapolisfed.org/article/2002/perfectly-competitive-superstars)
- Brynjolfsson, E., Hu, Y. (J.) & Smith, M. D. (2003). "Consumer Surplus in the Digital Economy: Estimating the Value of Increased Product Variety at Online Booksellers." *Management Science* 49(11): 1580–1596. DOI 10.1287/mnsc.49.11.1580.20580. Abstract figures: Amazon listed over 23 times more titles than a typical Barnes & Noble superstore and 57 times more than a typical large independent bookstore; increased variety raised consumer welfare by $731 million to $1.03 billion in 2000, which is 7 to 10 times the gain from increased competition and lower prices. This is a consumer-surplus result about the long tail, not a seller-earnings result. [ABSTRACT/RECORD] — [EconPapers record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:inm:ormnsc:v:49:y:2003:i:11:p:1580-1596)
- Garg, R. & Telang, R. (2013). "Inferring App Demand from Publicly Available Data." *MIS Quarterly* 37(4): 1253–1264. Setting: paid apps on Apple's App Store. Finding: the top-ranked paid iPhone app generates about 150 times more downloads than the paid app ranked 200th; on iPad the multiple is about 120. Demand measure is downloads, not revenue. [SNIPPET for the figures; record confirmed] — [AIS eLibrary record](https://aisel.aisnet.org/misq/vol37/iss4/14); [CMU working-paper record](https://kilthub.cmu.edu/articles/journal_contribution/ESTIMATING_APP_DEMAND_FROM_PUBLICLY_AVAILABLE_DATA/6471344)
- Bresnahan, T. F., Davis, J. P. & Yin, P.-L. (2014). "Economic Value Creation in Mobile Applications." Chapter in *The Changing Frontier: Rethinking Science and Innovation Policy* (University of Chicago Press / NBER). The abstract says the volume and diversity of app entry created marketing and commercialisation problems, especially matching consumers to products. I did not retrieve any revenue-distribution figures from this chapter. [ABSTRACT/RECORD] — [NBER chapter page](https://www.nber.org/chapters/c13044)
- Bresnahan, T. F., Greenstein, S. & Yin, P.-L. (2025). "New Economic Forces Behind the Value Distribution of Innovation." NBER Working Paper 34090 (August 2025; **not peer-reviewed**). Data: about 2,400 web and mobile products in the US, success measured in 2013. Findings: incremental co-invention accounts for 6% of total observed value; the upper tail of the value distribution is dominated by novel co-inventors; success from novel co-invention is "rare, challenging, and the source of the largest value". [FULL TEXT, first pages] — [NBER PDF](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w34090/w34090.pdf)
- Assabese, Celis & Destefanis (2026), Shopify app ecosystem (preprint; see A1): 74.8% of 16,698 active apps had zero detected installations; mean top-5 share within a category 71.2%. Measure is installations, not revenue. [FULL TEXT] — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.23771)
- Elberse, A. (2008). "Should You Invest in the Long Tail?" *Harvard Business Review*, July–August 2008. Existence confirmed at HBR; the article body was not retrievable. Secondary coverage reports: at Rhapsody the top 10% of titles accounted for 78% of all plays and the top 1% for 32%; at Quickflix (DVD rental) the top 10% accounted for 48% of rentals and the top 1% for 18%. [SNIPPET for figures] — [HBR article page](https://hbr.org/2008/07/should-you-invest-in-the-long-tail); [TechCrunch coverage](https://techcrunch.com/2008/07/02/poking-holes-in-the-long-tail-theory)
- Elberse, A. & Oberholzer-Gee, F., working paper "Superstars and Underdogs: An Examination of the Long-Tail Phenomenon in Video Sales" (US home video sales 2000–2005), as summarised by HBS Working Knowledge (23 Oct 2006): the number of titles in the top 10% of sales dropped by half; the number of titles selling only a few copies a week doubled; the number of titles selling nothing was four times its 2000 level. [Secondary summary by the authors' institution] — [HBS Working Knowledge](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/will-the-long-tail-work-for-hollywood)

**Industry analyses (not academic; modelled estimates)**
- ThemeForest, Freemius blog by Vova Feldman, published 7 Dec 2016, data from Envato's public API through 13 Nov 2016. Revenue is estimated as licences sold × list price; earnings assume a flat 30% commission. — [Freemius](https://freemius.com/blog/themeforest-wordpress-themes-analyisis/)
  - 28,644 items on ThemeForest, of which 7,986 (28%) were WordPress themes; lifetime transactions $355.6M since 2008, of which $286.4M (about 80.5%) from WordPress themes.
  - 76% of WordPress themes (6,098 of 7,986) earned under $1,000 a month; 5.6% over $3,000 a month; 0.83% over $10,000 a month; 3 themes over $100,000 a month.
  - 36.56% of WordPress themes had sold fewer than 100 licences; 86.9% fewer than 1,000; 0.5% more than 10,000.
  - 19 themes had passed $1M in sales; the top 20 WordPress themes took 13.5% ($18.76M) of annual WordPress theme sales; Avada alone over $16M gross.
  - Average WordPress theme price $50.63; most common price $59 (33% of themes); four price points ($59, $49, $44, $39) covered 81.3% of themes.
  - Average annual gross per WordPress theme $17,355 (a mean pulled up by the top sellers).
- Steam, We Love It / VaporLens "Steam Revenue Report" (industry, modelled from review counts at an assumed reviews-to-sales multiplier): top 1% of paid games took an estimated 84.5% of gross revenue across 99,206 paid games (84.8% across 105,052 in an updated version; 83.3% in another tracker that excludes games with no reviews); median lifetime gross about $3,453–$3,622. Valve publishes no sales data; the credible multiplier range is given as roughly 20× to 60×. [SNIPPET] — [We Love It report](https://weloveit.io/developers/steam-revenue-report/); [Notebookcheck coverage](https://www.notebookcheck.net/A-massive-Steam-economy-study-reveals-top-1-of-games-earn-nearly-85-of-all-revenue.1375617.0.html)

### Inferences
- The skew is consistent across every digital-goods catalogue examined (books, music, video, apps, Shopify apps, ThemeForest, Steam). By analogy, a new theme should be expected to land in the long tail by default; mean earnings figures (for example Freemius's $17,355 average annual gross) are misleading as an expectation because the median is far lower.
- The Freemius figures imply that in 2016 roughly 6% of WordPress themes on ThemeForest cleared $3,000 a month. That is a decade old and predates Envato Elements subscriptions, block themes and AI site generators, so it should be used only as a historical shape of the distribution, not as a current earnings estimate.
- The Bresnahan–Greenstein–Yin result (incremental products capture 6% of value) suggests by analogy that "another general-purpose theme" is an incremental product competing for a small share of total value, while novel products capture the upper tail. This is a loose analogy from web/mobile products in 2013.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study of revenue concentration on ThemeForest/Envato, the Shopify Theme Store, the WordPress.org theme directory, Webflow or Framer template marketplaces was found (searched Google-indexed scholarly sources, arXiv, ACM).
- No academic study of Steam revenue concentration, npm download concentration or VS Code extension install concentration was confirmed. One security-focused arXiv paper on the VS Code ecosystem ([arXiv:2411.07479](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.07479)) reports install statistics as a by-product, but the figures in the search summary were internally inconsistent and I did not verify them; do not cite numbers from it.
- "The Co-evolution of the WordPress Platform and Its Plugins" (ACM, DOI 10.1145/3533700; [ACM page](https://dl.acm.org/doi/full/10.1145/3533700)) exists and reportedly covers 60,592 plugins, but the page returned HTTP 403; it studies release/compatibility dynamics, not earnings.
- Elberse's HBR figures and Garg & Telang's rank multiples were not checked against the original article text.

## A3. Empirical studies specifically on theme/template marketplaces or CMS ecosystems

### Takeaway
I found no peer-reviewed empirical study of theme or template marketplace economics. The nearest academic work is the 2026 arXiv preprint on the Shopify app store and an unverified 2026 MDPI paper on PrestaShop monetisation; the only quantitative theme-seller evidence is the 2016 Freemius blog analysis of ThemeForest.

### Cited Findings
- Shopify app ecosystem study (Assabese, Celis & Destefanis, arXiv:2608.23771, Aug 2026, preprint): covers apps only; the paper's adoption data come from Store Leads storefront crawling. Shopify marketplace context given in the paper: over 16,000 active third-party apps, 2.7 million active merchant stores; median store installs 3 apps (mean 4.0); 19.1% of stores install zero apps. Merchant-side skew: median store estimated at $6,000 annual sales versus mean $261,435; 25th percentile $600; 99th percentile $4.0M (traffic-model estimates, not reported financials). [FULL TEXT] — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.23771)
- ThemeForest (Freemius, 2016, industry blog): see A2 for the distribution; the same analysis modelled a 10-year net of $113,598 for an average WordPress theme sold exclusively (Envato share falling from 50% to 30%) versus $62,407 non-exclusive (flat 64% Envato share) at the rates then in force. This is the author's projection from average sales, not observed earnings. — [Freemius](https://freemius.com/blog/themeforest-wordpress-themes-analyisis/)
- "Hybrid Monetization in an Open-Source Platform: Freemium, Data, and Value Capture in the PrestaShop Ecosystem," *Administrative Sciences* (MDPI) 16(6): 255, 2026, DOI 10.3390/admsci16060255. Existence confirmed via the DOI redirect to MDPI; the page returned HTTP 403, so authors, sample and findings are unverified. [SNIPPET] — [DOI link](https://doi.org/10.3390/admsci16060255)

### Inferences
- The merchant revenue skew in the Shopify data (median store about $6,000 a year in estimated sales) implies, by my reasoning, that most Shopify stores have little budget for a paid theme costing a few hundred dollars; the paying segment is a minority of the 3.35M stores counted in B2.
- Because the academic literature is empty here, the report should treat theme-marketplace profitability claims as resting on platform data and practitioner sources, not on research.

### Gaps
- No academic study of ThemeForest/Envato, the WordPress.org theme directory, the Shopify Theme Store, Webflow Templates or Framer Marketplace was found.
- The PrestaShop paper could not be opened; the WordPress plugin co-evolution paper could not be opened.
- I did not search IEEE Xplore directly (no direct access); coverage there relies on general web search.

## A4. Generative AI's measured impact on online freelance and creative/web-development work

### Takeaway
Published studies of the first year after ChatGPT find demand for substitutable freelance work fell by roughly 20–30%, with one Management Science paper measuring a 20.62% drop in job posts for software, website and app development; yet two other studies classify web development as complemented, one measuring a 66.5% earnings rise for web-development freelancers in 2023. All of this evidence ends in 2023 except the Stanford payroll study, which runs to June 2026 and covers employees rather than freelancers or theme sellers; no academic study yet measures the 2025–2026 AI site-builder wave.

### Cited Findings

**Hui, Reshef & Zhou**
- Hui, X., Reshef, O. & Zhou, L. (2024). "The Short-Term Effects of Generative Artificial Intelligence on Employment: Evidence from an Online Labor Market." *Organization Science* 35(6): 1977–1989. DOI 10.1287/orsc.2023.18441. [ABSTRACT/RECORD] — [WashU record](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/the-short-term-effects-of-generative-artificial-intelligence-on-e/); [INFORMS page](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/orsc.2023.18441)
- Working-paper version (CESifo WP 10601, Aug 2023) — numbers below are from this version, which I read; I did not confirm that the published version reports identical coefficients. Data: Upwork freelancers API, snapshot April 2023; period January 2022–April 2023; 92,547 freelancers offering service in a single occupation; treated group is writing-related occupations. [FULL TEXT] — [CESifo PDF](https://www.ifo.de/DocDL/cesifo1_wp10601.pdf)
  - After ChatGPT's release, freelancers in more affected (writing-related) occupations saw a 2% decrease in monthly number of jobs (s.e. 0.004) and a 5.2% decrease in monthly earnings (s.e. 0.016) relative to less affected occupations.
  - The probability of starting a new job in a month fell by 1.2 percentage points, about a 10% drop from baseline; conditional on employment, jobs fell about 4.7%.
  - For image-based generative AI, freelancers with above-median prior earnings saw reductions of 7% (jobs) and 14% (earnings), both significant at 1%.
  - High past quality did not moderate the adverse effects; there is suggestive evidence that top freelancers were disproportionately affected.
  - Context from the paper: on average a freelancer starts a job once every three months for an average monthly pay of $171.
- INFORMS press release for the published paper (4 Mar 2025): each 1% increase in a freelancer's past earnings was associated with an additional 0.5% drop in job opportunities and a 1.7% drop in monthly income after AI tools were introduced. [Publisher press release] — [INFORMS](https://www.informs.org/News-Room/INFORMS-Releases/News-Releases/Generative-AI-Is-Upending-Freelance-Work-Even-Top-Performers-Aren-t-Safe)

**Demirci, Hannane & Zhu — the only study with a web-development-specific demand estimate**
- Demirci, O., Hannane, J. & Zhu, X. (2025). "Who Is AI Replacing? The Impact of Generative AI on Online Freelancing Platforms." *Management Science* 71 (published online 24 Jan 2025). DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2024.05420. [ABSTRACT/RECORD] — [INFORMS page](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/mnsc.2024.05420); [Imperial repository](https://spiral.imperial.ac.uk/entities/publication/88113a91-b994-4717-8526-dadac657f1cb)
- Working-paper version (CESifo WP 11276, 2024) — numbers below are from this version, which I read; the published version was not checked for identical coefficients. Data: all job posts on "a leading global freelancing platform" from July 2021 to July 2023; final sample 1,218,463 job posts from 541,828 employers; fixed-payment jobs in the 61 largest countries. [FULL TEXT] — [CESifo PDF](https://www.ifo.de/DocDL/cesifo1_wp11276.pdf); [EconPapers record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:ces:ceswps:_11276)
  - Within eight months of ChatGPT's introduction, weekly job posts for automation-prone jobs fell 20.86% more than for manual-intensive jobs (abstract rounds this to 21%).
  - By cluster: writing −30.37%; **software, app and web development −20.62%**; engineering −10.42%.
  - Image-generating AI: −17.01% job posts for image creation overall; graphic design −18.49%; 3D modelling −15.57%.
  - Remaining automation-prone jobs: maximum budget +5.71%; average number of bids per job post +8.57%; job complexity (skill tags) +2.18%, relative to manual-intensive jobs.
  - The effect grew over time; a one-standard-deviation increase in Google search interest for ChatGPT in a job category was associated with an 8.01% decrease in job posts.

**Teutloff et al.**
- Teutloff, O., Einsiedler, J., Kässi, O., Braesemann, F., Mishkin, P. & del Rio-Chanona, R. M. (2025). "Winners and losers of generative AI: Early Evidence of Shifts in Freelancer Demand." *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization* 235: 106845. DOI 10.1016/j.jebo.2024.106845 (online 29 Jan 2025). Data: more than 3 million job postings from a leading online freelancing platform, daily postings January 2021–September 2023; 116 skill clusters labelled by GPT-4o as substitutable, complementary or unaffected. [FULL TEXT] — [Oxford ORA record](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:af491969-bee7-4b09-a696-2b500b6b21d8); [ORA PDF](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:af491969-bee7-4b09-a696-2b500b6b21d8/files/r8g84mp21z)
  - Demand increased after ChatGPT's launch only in complementary or unaffected clusters; demand for substitutable skills (writing, translation) fell 20–50% relative to the counterfactual trend, most sharply for short-term (1–3 week) jobs.
  - Within complementary clusters: machine-learning programming +24%; AI-powered chatbot development nearly tripled; demand for novice workers declined overall.
  - The paper states that in the complementary category the frequent skills are software-development related, naming web development, web design, API, JavaScript, HTML, CSS and PHP; graphic design fell in the unaffected category (text-only ChatGPT, Nov 2022 release).

**Liu, Xu, Nan, Li & Tan**
- Liu, J., Xu, X., Nan, X., Li, Y. & Tan, Y. "'Generate' the Future of Work through AI: Empirical Evidence from Online Labor Markets." arXiv:2308.05201 (v1 9 Aug 2023; v4 25 Jun 2026); INFORMS lists it in *Information Systems Research*, DOI 10.1287/isre.2023.0461 (publication year/volume not confirmed). Numbers below are from arXiv v4. Data: all job postings, bids, transactions and freelancer information from "one of the leading freelancer platforms", September 2021–August 2023; freelancer-level analysis on 132,260 freelancers. [FULL TEXT of arXiv v4] — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.05201); [INFORMS page](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/isre.2023.0461)
  - In treated (LLM-exposed, text- and programming-related) submarkets relative to controls after ChatGPT: job postings −22.1%; total bids −16.8%; total transaction volume −31.5%. Supply fell less than demand, so competition per job intensified, most strongly in programming-intensive submarkets.
  - Mechanism: ChatGPT lowered the human-capital barrier to programming, so incumbent freelancers moved into programming tasks; high-skilled freelancers drove the shift.
  - Freelancers relying exclusively on treated submarkets saw an 11.6% decline in monthly transaction value; those who stayed without transitioning saw a 20.8% reduction; those who made a skill transition realised an 11.75% increase in earnings per bid and avoided the loss in total earnings.

**Qiao, Rui & Xiong — the counter-finding for web development**
- Qiao, D., Rui, H. & Xiong, Q. "AI and Jobs: Has the Inflection Point Arrived? Evidence from an Online Labor Platform." arXiv:2312.04180 (v1 7 Dec 2023; v3 12 Dec 2025); a version titled "AI and Freelancers: Has the Inflection Point Arrived?" appeared at HICSS-58 (2025) and a journal version is listed by the *Journal of Management Information Systems* (volume/DOI not confirmed). Numbers below are from arXiv v3. Data: "one of the most popular freelance platforms", worker-month panel, May 2022–October 2023 (November–December 2022 excluded); 13,230 unique workers in the web-development market. [FULL TEXT of arXiv v3] — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.04180); [JMIS listing](https://jmis-web.org/articles/1739)
  - Translation & localisation: transaction volume −9.0%; earnings −29.7% after ChatGPT.
  - **Web development: transaction volume +6.4%; earnings +66.5%** after ChatGPT (January–October 2023 versus May–October 2022).
  - Extended window (May 2022–January 2025): the displacement effect in translation persists and intensifies; the productivity effect in web development "gradually diminishes and appears to taper off". The paper text dates this turning point to "after December 2023" and links it to OpenAI announcements it names as o1, Canvas and ChatGPT Pro; to my knowledge those products were announced in December 2024, so the paper's date may be a typo. Verify before quoting the date.
  - The paper's model: before an AI-capability "inflection point" workers benefit from AI improvement; beyond it they are replaced.

**Payroll employment (not freelance)**
- Brynjolfsson, E., Chandar, B. & Chen, R. "Canaries in the Coal Mine? Six Facts about the Recent Employment Effects of Artificial Intelligence." Stanford Digital Economy Lab working paper, first released August 2025, revised 12 August 2026. **Not peer-reviewed as retrieved.** Data: ADP payroll records covering millions of US workers, through June 2026. [FULL TEXT of Aug 2026 revision] — [Stanford Digital Economy Lab](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/publications/canaries-in-the-coal-mine); [Aug 2026 PDF](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/app/uploads/2026/08/Canaries_August2026.pdf)

  - No evidence of economy-wide displacement; adjustment runs through reduced hiring of young workers rather than separations, and through employment rather than base pay; declines concentrate where AI use substitutes for rather than complements tasks.
  - Software development and customer service are named as exposed fields. The authors call the results "early, descriptive indicators … rather than causal estimates".

**Usage and industry reports (not peer-reviewed)**

[SNIPPET — unverified] — [TechRadar](https://www.techradar.com/pro/the-collapse-of-web-coding-gigs-is-getting-ugly-ai-takes-over-routine-website-development-jobs-and-business-owners-are-very-happy-with-its-job-so-far)

### Inferences
- The two web-development results conflict on their face (Demirci et al.: −20.62% job posts; Qiao et al.: +66.5% earnings; Teutloff et al.: complementary). They are reconcilable: Demirci measures the number of employer job posts across a broad "software, app and web development" cluster; Qiao measures earnings of incumbent web-development freelancers who remained active. Fewer, larger, more complex jobs going to established workers fits both, and fits Demirci's own finding of higher budgets, more bids and higher complexity in the remaining jobs. This reconciliation is my reading, not a claim made by either paper.
- All freelance-platform evidence stops at 2023 (Qiao's extension to January 2025 shows the web-development gain tapering off). None of it measures the period after agentic coding tools and prompt-to-site builders became mainstream (2025–2026). Extrapolating the 2023 "web development is complemented" result to late 2026 is not supported.
- Custom freelance web work and pre-made themes are different products; none of these papers measured theme or template sales. By analogy, low-complexity, short-duration, easily specified work was hit hardest (Teutloff: 1–3 week jobs; Demirci: remaining jobs more complex), which suggests generic templates are more exposed than complex, integration-heavy builds. That is an analogy, not a measured result.
- The Liu et al. skill-transition mechanism (AI lowers the barrier to programming, so more freelancers enter) implies by analogy more entrants into theme building, which combined with A1's crowding results points to lower per-seller returns for undifferentiated themes.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed or working-paper study measuring the effect of AI website builders or "vibe coding" on freelance web-development demand, WordPress work or theme sales in 2025–2026 was found.
- The published versions of Hui et al. (Org. Science), Demirci et al. (Management Science) and Liu et al. (ISR) were not read; coefficients are from the CESifo/arXiv versions and may differ slightly from the published tables.
- The Freelancer.com −59% figure and the Bluehost survey could not be traced to primary documents.
- Qiao et al.'s journal volume and DOI were not confirmed.

## A5. Marketplace commission/fee, exclusivity and multi-homing literature

### Takeaway
The only direct evidence on a commission cut is from the Shopify app store preprint: reducing Shopify's revenue share to 0% below $1M in August 2021 produced no detectable change in developer entry or retention. No empirical study of seller profitability under exclusive versus non-exclusive marketplace rates (the Envato structure) was found; the exclusivity literature studies platforms' interests in video-game consoles, not sellers' earnings.

### Cited Findings
- Assabese, Celis & Destefanis (2026, arXiv preprint): the reduction of Shopify's platform commission to 0% below $1M, effective 1 August 2021, "produced no detectable change in entry": mean monthly new listings were 262.6 in the twelve months before and 261.2 in the twelve months after (a 0.5% decrease); the monthly exit hazard among tracked apps averaged 0.3% the year before and 0.4% the year after. The study does not observe developer profit. [FULL TEXT] — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.23771)
- Landsman, V. & Stremersch, S. (2011). "Multihoming in Two-Sided Markets: An Empirical Inquiry in the Video Game Console Industry." *Journal of Marketing* 75(6): 39–54. DOI 10.1509/jm.09.0199. Studies seller-level multihoming of games across consoles; later work summarises its main result as: higher sales shares of single-homed (exclusive) games, compared with multihomed games, increase the focal console's sales. That is a benefit to the platform, not a measured benefit to the seller. [SNIPPET for the finding; record confirmed] — [Erasmus repository](https://repub.eur.nl/pub/32867); [author PDF](https://personal.eur.nl/stremersch/artikels/Landsman_Stremersch_JM_2011_Print.pdf)
- Cennamo, C. & Santaló, J. (2013). "Platform competition: Strategic trade-offs in platform markets." *Strategic Management Journal*. DOI 10.1002/smj.2066. Setting: US video-game industry. Finding: when a platform pursues both a larger number/variety of applications and a larger fraction of exclusive applications with equal intensity, the benefits of each strategy diminish. [ABSTRACT/RECORD] — [Wiley page](https://sms.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/smj.2066)
- Agarwal et al., "A platform for change? Core component innovations, complementor adaptation, and multihoming in platform-based ecosystems," *Strategic Management Journal*, DOI 10.1002/smj.70136: using iOS release data, complementors increase co-innovation on iOS while becoming more likely to multihome to the competing platform. Full author list and year not retrieved. [SNIPPET] — [Wiley page](https://sms.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/smj.70136)
- Jeon, D.-S. & Rey, P., "Platform Competition and Innovation" / "Platform Competition and App Development," TSE Working Paper 1566 (2024): theoretical model in which, when one platform's commission reduces the number of apps on the rival platform, competition generates higher commissions. Theory; no empirical estimate. [SNIPPET] — [TSE working paper](https://www.tse-fr.eu/sites/default/files/TSE/documents/doc/wp/2024/wp_tse_1566.pdf)
- Llanes, G. & Madio, L., "App Store Competition" (working paper): developers' incentive to earn complementary revenues leads to a negative pass-through of commission fees to app prices. Working paper; theory. [SNIPPET] — [Georgetown KGI copy](https://kgi.georgetown.edu/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/App-Store-Competition_Llanes-Madio_61.pdf)
- Apple-commissioned Analysis Group report (May 2022) on the App Store Small Business Program (commission 15% instead of 30% for developers with up to $1M in prior-year proceeds, launched January 2021): describes the cut as equivalent to a 21% increase in earnings for eligible developers; this is the report's arithmetic, not a measured causal effect, and the report is funded by the platform. [Industry-commissioned report] — [Apple newsroom PDF](https://www.apple.com/newsroom/pdfs/app-store-small-business-report-25-may-2022.pdf); [Apple programme page](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/small-business-program/)
- Freemius (2016, industry blog): at Envato's then-current rates, modelled 10-year net for an average WordPress theme was $113,598 exclusive (Envato share 50% falling to 30% with volume) versus $62,407 non-exclusive (flat 64% Envato share). A projection from average sales that assumes identical sales under both options and ignores revenue from other channels. — [Freemius](https://freemius.com/blog/themeforest-wordpress-themes-analyisis/)
- Hui, Reshef & Zhou's working paper notes, citing Horton (2017), that multi-homing by freelancers on Upwork is rare. Secondary citation inside a paper; not about theme sellers. [FULL TEXT] — [CESifo PDF](https://www.ifo.de/DocDL/cesifo1_wp10601.pdf)

### Inferences
- The Shopify result (no entry or retention response to a commission cut from the standard rate to 0% below $1M) suggests by analogy that the commission rate is not the binding constraint for small sellers; demand and discoverability are. A seller with near-zero sales gains nothing from a lower fee. This is my interpretation; the paper measures entry and exit, not profit.
- The exclusivity literature shows exclusivity benefits the platform (Landsman & Stremersch). By analogy, Envato's higher author share for exclusive sellers is the price the platform pays for that benefit; whether it compensates a theme author for giving up other channels is an empirical question no study has answered.
- The Freemius exclusive-vs-non-exclusive model holds sales constant across the two options, so it mechanically favours exclusivity; it says nothing about the incremental revenue from multi-homing on a second marketplace or the author's own site.

### Gaps
- No empirical study of seller profitability under exclusive versus non-exclusive commission tiers on a digital-goods marketplace (Envato, stock media, template stores) was found.
- No independent causal study of the Apple or Google small-business commission reductions on developer outcomes was found in this search.
- No study of multi-homing by theme sellers across ThemeForest, own-site sales and platform-native stores was found.
- Current Envato, Shopify Theme Store, Webflow and Framer commission terms are outside this brief (covered by other researchers); the 2016 Envato rates above are historical.

## A6. Pricing of digital goods and freemium conversion research

### Takeaway
I could not confirm a peer-reviewed measured freemium conversion rate for themes or plugins. The academic literature supports two narrower points: a well-rated free version raises sales of the paid version (Google Play), and extending the premium line raises premium revenue (a publisher field experiment, +8.9%); the commonly quoted 2–5% conversion range appears in an academic institution's summary but was not verified in a paper.

### Cited Findings
- Liu, C. Z., Au, Y. A. & Choi, H. S. "Effects of Freemium Strategy in the Mobile App Market: An Empirical Study of Google Play." *Journal of Management Information Systems* 31(3): 326–354. DOI 10.1080/07421222.2014.995564 (listed as 2014 in one index and March 2015 in another). Panel of 711 ranked mobile apps. Findings: offering a free version is associated with higher sales of the paid app; a higher review rating of the free version raises paid-version sales, while the free version's sales rank has no significant effect. [ABSTRACT/RECORD] — [Georgia Southern record](https://scholars.georgiasouthern.edu/en/publications/effects-of-freemium-strategy-in-the-mobile-app-market-an-empirica-3/); [JMIS listing](https://www.jmis-web.org/articles/1219)
- Gu, X., Kannan, P. K. & Ma, L. (2018). "Selling the Premium in Freemium." *Journal of Marketing* 82(6): 10–27. DOI 10.1177/0022242918807170. Randomised field experiment at the National Academies Press (free PDF, paid paperback): adding a premium hardback to the line increased paperback revenue by 8.9%; the publisher's summary cautions that a lower-priced premium option can cannibalise the existing premium product. [SNIPPET for the 8.9%; record confirmed] — [AMA summary](https://www.ama.org/2018/12/06/winning-the-freemium-to-premium-product-wars/); [University of Maryland summary](https://www.rhsmith.umd.edu/research/getting-you-pay-freemium-world)
- Deng, Y., Lambrecht, A. & Liu, Y., "Spillover Effects and Freemium Strategy in the Mobile App Market," *Management Science*, DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2022.4619. Existence confirmed at INFORMS; findings not retrieved. A UCL School of Management post about this research states it is "not uncommon" for freemium products to have conversion rates between two and five percent; the UCL page returned HTTP 403 on direct fetch, so this range is from a search summary. [SNIPPET] — [INFORMS page](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/mnsc.2022.4619); [UCL repository copy](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/10161666/1/Deng%2C%20Lambrecht%20and%20Liu%20forthcoming.pdf)
- Stanford GSB working paper "Price Promotions in Freemium Settings" (randomised experiment in a free-to-play game): promotions increased conversion by 37% across onset times. This is a relative lift, not a conversion level; authors not retrieved. [SNIPPET] — [Stanford GSB](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/index%2Ephp/faculty-research/working-papers/price-promotions-freemium-settings)
- "Willingness to pay for freemium services: Addressing the differences between monetization strategies," *International Journal of Information Management* (2024): a meta-analysis drawing on 55 studies and 15 interviews. Findings not retrieved. [SNIPPET] — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0268401224000355)
- Freemius (2016): observed ThemeForest WordPress theme price points clustered at $59 (33%), $49 (26.1%), $44 (14.6%) and $39 (7.4%), average $50.63. Historical industry data. — [Freemius](https://freemius.com/blog/themeforest-wordpress-themes-analyisis/)

### Inferences
- By analogy from Liu, Au & Choi: for a freemium theme, the rating/quality of the free version matters more for paid upgrades than its download rank. Tested on Google Play apps, not themes.
- By analogy from Gu, Kannan & Ma: offering more than one paid tier above a free theme may lift revenue from the main paid tier. Tested on books.
- The 2–5% conversion range should be presented in the report as a commonly cited rule of thumb, not a research finding for themes.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed measured free-to-paid conversion rate for WordPress themes or plugins was found. A practitioner blog claims 1–3% for WordPress plugins ([Gatilab](https://gatilab.com/economics-of-freemium/)); it gives no verifiable data and should not be cited as research.
- No academic study of theme price elasticity, subscription-vs-one-time pricing for themes, or the effect of bundle subscriptions (for example Envato Elements) on author revenue was found.

## B3. HTTP Archive Web Almanac (latest edition: 2025, published January 2026)

### Takeaway
The 2025 Web Almanac puts WordPress at 64.3% of CMS-driven sites on mobile with Shopify at 7.3% and Wix at 5.2%, and reports that WordPress growth had slowed to under one point and slipped from its 2024 peak. Its page-builder data show Elementor falling from about 56% to 43% of WordPress page-builder detections as the native Block Editor rose to about 18%.

### Cited Findings
- Web Almanac 2025, CMS chapter (published 15 Jan 2026, updated 16 Jan 2026; written by Vahe Arabian; analysts Onur Güler and Alon Kochba; crawl date not stated in the chapter; source type: primary crawl analysis) — [Web Almanac 2025 CMS](https://almanac.httparchive.org/en/2025/cms):
  - CMS adoption 2021 → 2025: desktop 42% → 55%; mobile 43% → 54%.
  - Share of CMS-driven sites (mobile): WordPress 64.3%; Shopify 7.3% (text also gives up to 7.8%); Wix 5.2%; Squarespace about 3%; Joomla below 3%; Webflow and Drupal each under 2%.

  - By rank: among the top 10,000 sites WordPress is roughly 58% of CMS usage and Drupal about 6–7%; Wix and Shopify are almost absent at that level; Shopify peaks at 5.0% among the top 10 million.
  - Mobile CMS adoption by country: Italy 51%, United States and United Kingdom 49% each, Brazil 32%, Indonesia 25%.
  - WordPress page builders: about 60% of WordPress sites use one (estimate). Elementor 43% mobile / 42% desktop (down from about 56% in 2024); WordPress Block Editor about 18% (up); WPBakery 13% (down from about 21%); Divi 10% (down from about 14%); Beaver Builder about 2%.

- Web Almanac 2025, Ecommerce chapter (published 15 Jan 2026; written and analysed by Amandeep Singh) — [Web Almanac 2025 Ecommerce](https://almanac.httparchive.org/en/2025/ecommerce):

### Inferences
- The Almanac (2025 crawl) and W3Techs (November 2026) are not comparable point-in-time: different site samples, different denominators (Almanac shares are of CMS-driven sites; W3Techs headline shares are of all sites) and about a year apart. ### Gaps
- The 2026 edition of the Web Almanac had not been published as far as I could find; the 2025 edition is the latest.
- The Almanac does not report theme usage or paid-theme shares.
- The exact crawl month for the 2025 chapters was not stated on the pages fetched.

## B5. Published market-size figures for website templates/themes, and their reliability

### Takeaway
I found no reputable, methodologically transparent market-size figure for website templates or themes. Commercial "market research" estimates for the broader website-builder market in 2026 range from $3.57 billion to $18.37 billion, a fivefold spread that shows they should not be cited as fact.

### Cited Findings
- No templates/themes-specific market-size report from a verifiable source was found in this search (retrieved 2026-11-24).
- Website-builder market, 2026 estimates from commercial report vendors (source type: paywalled syndicated market research; methodology not disclosed in what was retrievable):
  - Mordor Intelligence: $3.57 billion in 2026 (from $3.06 billion in 2025); the vendor says figures come from its proprietary estimation framework. [SNIPPET] — [Mordor Intelligence](https://www.mordorintelligence.com/industry-reports/website-builders-market)
  - Grand View Research: $4.7 billion in 2025, about $5.3 billion in 2026. [SNIPPET] — [Grand View Research](https://www.grandviewresearch.com/industry-analysis/website-builder-market-report)
  - ResearchAndMarkets: $5.40 billion in 2026. [SNIPPET] — [ResearchAndMarkets](https://www.researchandmarkets.com/report/website-builder)
  - Next Move Strategy Consulting: $11.57 billion in 2025, $18.37 billion in 2026. [SNIPPET] — [Next MSC](https://www.nextmsc.com/report/website-builder-software-market-ic6202)
- The only data-derived size figure for a theme marketplace found: Freemius's API-based estimate of ThemeForest gross merchandise value — $355.6 million lifetime (2008 to 13 Nov 2016), with a projection of $175.7 million for 2017, of which $138.6 million WordPress themes. Estimated as licences × list price; a decade old; the 2017 figure was a projection, not an outcome. — [Freemius](https://freemius.com/blog/themeforest-wordpress-themes-analyisis/)

### Inferences
- The fivefold disagreement among vendors for the same year, the absence of disclosed methods and the fact that these figures cover builder subscriptions rather than theme sales mean none of them sizes theme demand. The report should say that no reliable market-size figure for themes exists and rely on the installed-base proxies in B1–B3 instead.
- The Freemius figure is the only bottom-up number, and it suggests that at its 2016–2017 scale ThemeForest was a market of the order of $150–200 million a year in gross sales. Whether it has grown or shrunk since is unknown from this research.

### Gaps
- No current (2024–2026) gross sales figure for ThemeForest/Envato Market, the Shopify Theme Store, Webflow Templates or Framer Marketplace was found in this brief's scope; company filings or platform disclosures (covered by other researchers) would be the right source.
- I did not open any of the paywalled market-research reports; their scope definitions and methods are unverified.
